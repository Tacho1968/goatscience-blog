A 7,000-year-old goatskin: where did Iberia's first goats come from?

DNA from a tanned Neolithic hide found in a cave in Granada, Spain, shows it came from a domestic goat of European origin, most closely related to the Bermeya breed of northern Spain.

What did they ask?

Cueva de los Murciélagos, in Albuñol (Granada, southern Spain), is famous for preserving organic materials that almost never survive: baskets, cords, clothing. The cave’s dry environment has desiccated and protected them for thousands of years. It was used as a burial site in several phases of prehistory.

During a recent survey, a tanned Capra hide with hand-made perforations turned up among the debris left by 19th-century miners. The researchers asked two questions: did it come from a domestic goat or from the Iberian ibex? And if it was domestic, how was it related to the goats living in Iberia today?

Until now there were no nuclear genetic data from Neolithic Iberian goats.

What did they do?

A Spanish-Irish team, working with the ancient DNA laboratory at Trinity College Dublin, analysed two fragments of the hide, about 35 × 20 cm and 23 × 14 cm.

  • They radiocarbon dated it.
  • They examined it under the microscope to tell human-made perforations from insect damage and to confirm tanning.
  • They extracted and sequenced its DNA, with the usual precautions for ancient DNA.
  • They compared that DNA with modern goats from around the world (the VarGoats dataset), with the Iberian ibex and with previously published Neolithic goats from Serbia and Iran.

What did they find?

It is about 7,000 years old. The hide dates to between 7,250 and 7,000 years ago, in the earliest Neolithic phase of the cave. It is contemporary with bowstrings made from goat sinew found at the same site. It was probably part of a burial assemblage, perhaps as a garment or wrapping.

It was a domestic goat, and female. Its DNA is much closer to modern domestic breeds than to the Iberian ibex. Its maternal lineage is haplogroup A, the most common in European goats.

Its ancestry was European, not African. In the genetic analyses it groups with southern European breeds (Swiss, Italian, French). Of the modern breeds compared, the closest relative is the Bermeya, from Asturias in northern Spain. By contrast, it shows no affinity with African breeds.

Southern Spanish breeds have changed since then. Many modern Spanish breeds carry some African ancestry. The Palmera, from La Palma in the Canary Islands, for example, groups with African breeds. The Albuñol goat lacked that signature, suggesting African influence arrived after the Early Neolithic.

There was also human and fox DNA. The human DNA fits with someone handling or wearing the hide. The fox DNA appears much more recent and probably comes from foxes that entered the cave later.

Why does it matter?

These are the first nuclear genetic data from domestic goats of the Iberian Neolithic, and one of the oldest genomes recovered from soft tissue outside frozen or high-altitude settings.

For anyone who works with goats, it is especially interesting: it shows that part of the ancestry of today’s breeds, particularly in the north, comes directly from the first herds that reached Iberia about 7,000 years ago. It also helps to date the arrival of the African influence seen today in many breeds of southern Spain and the islands.

And it is a good reminder of the value of conserving local breeds: they are a genetic heritage with thousands of years of history.

Limitations

  • A single specimen. Everything rests on one animal, which cannot be generalised to all Neolithic Iberian goats.
  • Very little DNA. Genome coverage is very low, with only about 9,000 usable genetic positions. As a result, some comparisons with particular breeds do not reach significance.
  • Few Spanish breeds in the comparison. Only four were available as references (Bermeya, one Catalan, one Balearic and the Palmera). With more local breeds, the relationships might look different.
  • The timing of African influence is an inference. It rests on the absence of an African signal in this single, low-coverage goat, so more samples are needed to confirm it.
