## What did they ask?

*Staphylococcus aureus* is one of the important causes of mastitis in dairy goats, both clinical and subclinical. It lowers milk yield and quality, harms animal welfare and costs money. It is also a bacterium that colonises and infects people.

It is known to live in goats' noses and on their skin, but little was known about where else it is found on a farm and how it moves from one place to another. The authors, from Utrecht University and Wageningen (the Netherlands), set out to find out by looking at animals, people and the environment at the same time, an approach known as *One Health*.

## What did they do?

Between February and May 2025 they visited 29 dairy goat farms across the country, with an average of about 1,100 goats in milk. They took 329 samples of four kinds:

- the noses of 5 goats per farm (145 samples),
- the hands of 1 to 4 workers or residents per farm (80 samples),
- milking parlour teat cups, inside and outside (54 samples),
- and airborne dust, collected with electrostatic dust collectors hung for about 5–7 days above the barn and above the milking parlour (50 samples).

They cultured the samples, identified the bacterium and sequenced the whole genome of the isolates. Sequencing shows whether bacteria from different places are the same strain.

## What did they find?

**It is on almost every farm.** *S. aureus* was found on 27 of the 29 farms, and about 27% of all samples were positive. Within farms, the share of positive samples ranged from 0% to 58%.

**Airborne dust was the reservoir with the most positives.** More than half of the dust samples, from both the barn and the milking parlour, carried the bacterium. That is significantly more than goats' noses, hands or teat cups. Hands and teat cups were positive least often.

**The same strains in goats, people and the environment.** Sequencing showed that the dominant strain was ST133, the most typical of small ruminants (77% of isolates), followed by ST398 (10%). Isolates from noses, hands, teat cups and dust on the same farm were usually very closely related. Between farms, by contrast, they were rarely shared: of the 14 genetic clusters found, 10 occurred on a single farm.

**Contact with goats and exposure.** People who neither entered the barns nor handled the animals did not carry the bacterium on their hands. Most participants did not wear gloves.

## Why does it matter?

The practical message is that farmers should assume *S. aureus* is present on the farm, even when there are no visible clinical cases. Subclinical mastitis is hard to detect and the bacterium can circulate unnoticed.

The study also suggests that mastitis control is not only about treating goats: the environment matters. Dust could be a transmission route between animals and to people. The authors therefore recommend strengthening hygiene, environmental management and milking routines, and studying less dusty bedding and better ventilation.

That strains circulate mainly within each farm is also good news: measures taken on each farm can make a difference.

## Limitations

- **Volunteer farms.** The farms that took part had signed up because they were interested in udder health, so they may not represent the whole sector.
- **Samples not comparable over time.** Dust was collected over 5–7 days, while nose, hand and teat-cup swabs capture a single moment. This favours more positives in dust.
- **The statistics did not account for the farm.** Samples from all farms were pooled without considering that samples from the same farm resemble each other, as the authors themselves point out.
- **Only hands were sampled in people**, not noses, which are the most common human reservoir. Presence in workers is probably underestimated.
- **Culture can miss the bacterium** when other species overgrow the plate, so the method's sensitivity is uncertain.
- **It is a cross-sectional study**: it shows where the bacterium is at one point in time, but does not prove how it is transmitted.
