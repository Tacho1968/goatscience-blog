## What did they ask?

Paratuberculosis is a chronic enteritis caused by *Mycobacterium avium* subsp. *paratuberculosis* (Map). It progresses slowly, reduces production and increases mortality in herds. Vaccination is the best tool to control clinical disease, but current vaccines, given subcutaneously, have drawbacks: protection is incomplete, they leave a granuloma at the injection site and they interfere with bovine tuberculosis diagnosis.

In an earlier study, the same group from the University of León and CSIC (Spain) compared two experimental vaccines in goats, one oral and one intradermal, with the commercial subcutaneous vaccine (Gudair®). The commercial vaccine protected best, but the oral vaccine reduced lesion severity and the intradermal one reduced bacterial load.

This time they wanted to know what happens where the battle is actually fought: the gut mucosa. Does each vaccination route leave a different mark on the local immune response?

## What did they do?

They worked with 28 one-month-old goat kids from the earlier study:

- 6 received the oral vaccine,
- 6 the intradermal vaccine,
- 6 the commercial subcutaneous vaccine,
- and 10 were left unvaccinated.

One month later, half of each vaccinated group (3 animals) and 7 of the unvaccinated kids were experimentally infected with Map. Ten months after infection, all animals were euthanised and a Peyer's patch from the distal jejunum was analysed, the area with the most lesions and bacteria.

They counted macrophages and their two main types (M1, more inflammatory, and M2, more regulatory), γδ T cells and IgA-producing plasma cells. They also analysed the expression of several cytokines, the response of gut cells when re-exposed to the antigen, and Map-specific IgA in the intestinal contents. Lesions were classed as focal (small, localised granulomas), multifocal or diffuse (granulomas spread throughout the mucosa).

## What did they find?

**The vaccination route mattered little.** In uninfected animals, the gut response was very similar with any vaccine or none. The only exception was IL-17A, an inflammatory cytokine, which was increased in all vaccinated groups.

**Lesion severity mattered most.** When animals were grouped by lesion type, clear differences emerged:

- **Focal lesions:** the profile pointed to a Th1-type response, the kind linked to controlling infection. M1 macrophages and γδ T cells clustered around the granulomas, and the local interferon-gamma response was higher.
- **Diffuse lesions:** there were many more macrophages, especially of the M2 type, which is considered more permissive to the bacterium. When stimulated, the cells produced more IL-4, consistent with a response starting to shift towards the Th2 type, which is less effective against mycobacteria.

**Gut IgA tracked the infection.** Map-specific IgA in the jejunal contents was higher in infected animals. It correlated with bacterial load in faeces (ρ = 0.76) and in the Peyer's patch (ρ = 0.69), and with serum IgG (ρ = 0.68). In animals with focal lesions, by contrast, it was barely different from uninfected animals.

**The oral vaccine stood apart.** Kids vaccinated orally and then infected expressed less IL-1β and IL-17A and had fewer macrophages. The authors suggest this could explain why their lesions were milder despite a high bacterial load. But they caution that this does not mean good infection control: the oral vaccine seems to decouple inflammation from bacterial burden without clearing the bacterium.

## Why does it matter?

Finding paratuberculosis vaccines that do not interfere with tuberculosis diagnosis or leave granulomas is a priority. Oral and intradermal routes are candidates, but what they do in the ruminant gut had hardly been studied.

This work carries two messages. First, in the long run the gut response is shaped mainly by how infection progresses in each animal, more than by the vaccine received. So when comparing vaccines in experimental studies, it helps to group animals by lesion type. Second, fewer lesions do not always mean fewer bacteria, as the oral vaccine shows.

In addition, specific IgA in the jejunal contents could serve, under experimental conditions, as a marker of disease progression or vaccine efficacy.

## Limitations

- **Very few animals per group.** Only 3 vaccinated and infected kids per route, with high variability between individuals. The authors acknowledge this limits conclusions about each vaccine's effect.
- **A single sampling time.** Everything was measured ten months after infection. The vaccine's initial effect may have faded by then, and how the response evolved over time is unknown.
- **Experimental infection.** Conditions do not fully reproduce natural infection in a herd.
- **One part of the gut.** The study looked at the distal jejunal Peyer's patch, the most affected site in this model, but not other parts of the intestine.
